Arabic SEO is not translated SEO: a field guide

Search intent, dialect and script: what actually ranks in Egyptian and regional Arabic search, and how to structure a bilingual site so both languages can rank.

Arabic SEO is not translated SEO. Give Arabic pages their own URLs linked with hreflang, research keywords in dialect, standard Arabic, English and Franco, use the most searched spellings, write natively from the research, and structure pages so AI answer engines can quote them.

Last updated 16 October 2026

Many bilingual websites in the region are built in English and then translated. The Arabic version often cannot be indexed, targets words nobody searches for, and reads like a machine wrote it. Arabic SEO starts with how people actually search.

1. Give Arabic its own URLs

If your language switch only changes text with JavaScript on the same URL, search engines see one language. Use separate URLs such as /ar/ for Arabic pages, add hreflang tags that link each page to its twin, and include both versions in your sitemap.

2. Research in dialect, not only in Fusha

People in Egypt search for many services in Egyptian Arabic, while others use Modern Standard Arabic or English. Some even type Arabic words in Latin letters. Research every variant and choose per page.

  • Egyptian: “شركة تسويق” and “عايز اعمل اعلان”.
  • Standard: “وكالة تسويق رقمي”.
  • Franco: “sherket marketing”.

3. Mind the script

Arabic search treats variants like أ and ا, or ة and ه, inconsistently. Use the most searched spelling in titles and headings, and natural variants in the body text.

4. Write natively

Translated pages keep English sentence structure and miss the questions Arabic speakers actually ask. Brief Arabic writers with the research, not with the English page.

5. Prepare for AI answers

AI assistants increasingly answer Arabic questions directly. Clear facts, structured data and short, direct answers make it more likely that your page is the one they cite.

A bilingual architecture checklist

  • Separate URLs for each language, such as /ar/, never a JavaScript switch on the same address.
  • hreflang tags on every page linking it to its twin, plus an x-default.
  • The html lang and dir attributes set correctly, so Arabic pages render right to left.
  • Both languages in the sitemap, each with its alternates.
  • Arabic titles, descriptions and headings written for Arabic searches, not translated.

Choosing dialect page by page

There is no single right register for a whole site. A service page for Egyptian consumers may rank and convert best in Egyptian Arabic, a B2B page may need Modern Standard Arabic or English, and a page for Saudi customers needs Saudi phrasing. Let the keyword research decide each page, then keep the tone consistent within it.

Spelling variants without stuffing

Arabic search handles letters such as أ and ا, ة and ه, or ي and ى inconsistently, and people type them differently. Put the most searched spelling in the title and main heading, use natural variants once or twice in the body, and never repeat a phrase just to catch a spelling. Readers notice stuffing before search engines do.

Writing that AI answers can quote

Answer engines quote short, self-contained passages. Open each page with a direct summary of what it is, who it is for and where. Make every FAQ answer repeat its subject instead of starting with it or this. Define terms in one or two sentences, keep company facts identical everywhere, and add structured data such as FAQPage and BreadcrumbList.

Arabic page titles and descriptions

Write Arabic titles for the Arabic search, not as a translation of the English title. Put the main phrase people search near the start, keep the brand at the end, and stay within about 30 to 65 characters so the title is not cut. Descriptions work best as one or two plain sentences that answer what the page offers and who it is for, within about 70 to 160 characters.

Internal links between the two languages

Every Arabic page should link to other Arabic pages, not back to English ones, so search engines and readers stay in one language. The language switch should lead to the matching page in the other language, not to the homepage. Breadcrumbs, navigation and related links all need Arabic labels written for Arabic readers.

Measuring Arabic SEO separately

Report Arabic and English results separately: impressions, clicks and positions for the /ar/ pages in Search Console, and leads or sales from Arabic visitors in analytics. When the two are blended, a strong English site can hide an Arabic version that is not indexed or not ranking, which is exactly the problem a bilingual architecture is meant to solve.

Structured data in Arabic

Structured data tells search engines and AI systems what a page is: an organization, a service, an article, a FAQ, a breadcrumb trail. On a bilingual site, the Arabic page should carry structured data in Arabic with its own URL, and its language set to Arabic, matching what is visible on the page. Mismatched or English-only markup on Arabic pages wastes the signal.

Keep company facts identical in both languages: name, address, phone, founders and services. Answer engines trust facts that are stated the same way everywhere.

The takeaway

Treat Arabic as a market, not a translation. Separate URLs, dialect-aware research and native writing are the foundation.

Key terms

Terms used in this article.

hreflang
hreflang tags tell search engines which page is the Arabic version and which is the English one, so each searcher lands on the right language.
Technical SEO
Technical SEO makes a site easy to crawl, index and understand: clean URLs, speed, structured data, sitemaps and no duplicate pages competing with each other.
AI search optimization (GEO)
AI search optimization prepares content to be found, understood and quoted by answer engines such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews: clear answers, defined terms and consistent facts.
